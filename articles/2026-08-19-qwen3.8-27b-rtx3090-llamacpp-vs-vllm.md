# Qwen3.8-27B: 98 tok/s, 8 users at once, one old RTX 3090 — the Chinese did it

_2026-08-19_

**98 tokens/second from a 27B on a five-year-old consumer GPU — and eight
requests served at once at 409 tok/s combined.** The same model, on the same
card, through llama.cpp: 57 tok/s, one request at a time. This post is the
head-to-head, measured on my box with one harness, plus the two data points
nobody published: what the stack does at 350 W, and whether the quant kept its
eyes.

**TL;DR**

- Qwen3.8-27B (Apache 2.0, out Aug 14) on one RTX 3090, both engines, same
  harness and prompts.
- The [syv-ai vLLM stack](https://github.com/syv-ai/qwen38-27b-rtx3090):
  **92–98 tok/s** single-stream, **387–409 tok/s across 8 streams**, 131k
  context, first token in 167 ms. Independently verified here — it's real.
- llama.cpp, same model as GGUF + MTP: 54–57 tok/s, single stream only.
- **Check your GPU power limit.** Mine sat at 200 W; lifting it to 350 W was
  worth +45–70% — more than most software tuning.
- The W4A16 AutoRound quant is ~lossless _and_ kept the vision tower (the
  launch script just disables it — one flag).
- Full reproduction: [the guide](2026-08-19-guide-qwen3.8-27b-vllm-rtx3090.md).

**Credit where it's due: the clever parts are all
[syv-ai's](https://github.com/syv-ai/qwen38-27b-rtx3090)** — the patches, the
requantization, the draft-vocabulary idea. My contribution is independent
verification on different conditions (350 W, shared desktop GPU) and this
write-up. Numbers below are second-pass measurements per their repo's own
protocol.

## Results

tok/s end-to-end, model-default sampling (greedy in parens where it differs):

|                     | llama.cpp @200 W | llama.cpp @350 W | vLLM @200 W | vLLM @350 W |
| ------------------- | ---------------- | ---------------- | ----------- | ----------- |
| 1 stream            | 31               | 54 (57)          | 64–65       | **92 (98)** |
| 2 streams total     | —                | —                | 109         | 172–180     |
| 4 streams total     | —                | —                | 168         | 290–299     |
| 8 streams total     | —                | —                | 245         | **387–409** |
| mean TTFT, 1 stream | —                | 370–410 ms       | 250 ms      | **167 ms**  |

**Against syv-ai's own published numbers** (their measurements, RTX 3090 at
250 W, same `CTX=long` config — mine at 350 W):

|                 | mine @200 W | syv-ai published @250 W | mine @350 W |
| --------------- | ----------- | ----------------------- | ----------- |
| 1 stream        | 64–65 tok/s | 83–87                   | 92–98       |
| 8 streams total | 245 tok/s   | 328–364                 | 387–409     |

So the verification holds with margin: more watts, more tokens, same stack.
(Their 64k "fast" mode headline is 114–124 tok/s at 250 W; I only ran that mode
at 200 W, where it did 62 — not directly comparable, so it stays out of the
table.)

MTP acceptance was healthy everywhere (2.6–2.9 tokens per step), so the gap is
not the speculation — it's kernels plus batching.

**What "N streams" means in practice.** A stream is one in-flight request —
not necessarily a person. Run a coding agent and it's the agent's turn, a
subagent's search, and your chat question all landing at once; three streams.
llama.cpp with `--parallel 1` serves exactly one — everything else waits in
line (or, if you orchestrate multiple models, triggers a swap; that way lies
misery). vLLM at 8 streams delivered 387–409 tok/s _total_, which is ~50 tok/s
_per stream_ — every one of the eight still individually about as fast as the
single llama.cpp stream, and nobody queues. That, more than the single-stream
number, is what changes daily life with agents.

**GPU RAM vs system RAM.** Serving lives almost entirely in VRAM: vLLM pins
~21–22 GB of the card (weights + KV pool + recurrent states, sized by
`--gpu-memory-utilization`), llama.cpp about the same at 196k context. System
RAM is modest at steady state (a few GB of process overhead) — with two
caveats. Loading streams the whole checkpoint (14.7 GB here) through RAM, so a
~30 GiB box wants nothing big resident during startup — my first attempts got
OOM-killed because another server was holding a large host-side prompt cache.
And llama.cpp's optional `--cache-ram` feature deliberately trades system RAM
(gigabytes of it) for fast conversation switching; that's a knob you chose, not
a hidden cost.

Three things I did not expect:

1. **The power limit was my biggest "optimization."** My card was capped at
   200 W (leftover from a stability hunt). Lifting it to 350 W bought +70% on
   llama.cpp and +45% on vLLM single-stream. Check `nvidia-smi -q -d POWER`
   before you tune anything else.
2. **Long context is nearly free on this architecture, and fp8-KV long mode
   beat the "fast" 64k mode under load.** 48 of Qwen3.8's 64 layers are Gated
   DeltaNet with a fixed ~150 MB/request recurrent state; only 16 layers pay
   real KV. The syv-ai `CTX=long` (131k) config matched `CTX=fast` (64k) at 1
   stream and beat it at 8 on my card.
3. **At 350 W a stock-ish 3090 exceeds the repo's published numbers** (they
   measured at 250 W): 92 vs their 83 tok/s single-stream on the same config.

## Setups

|                 | llama.cpp                        | vLLM                                                                                            |
| --------------- | -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| build / version | b4df29be4 (Aug 16)               | 0.27.1 + syv-ai's 8 patches                                                                     |
| quant           | unsloth UD-Q3_K_XL (13 GiB GGUF) | dbirks W4A16 AutoRound, int8-requantized embeddings/lm_head, int4-GPTQ drafter ("fast variant") |
| KV cache        | q4_0, 196k context               | fp8 (FlashInfer), 131k context                                                                  |
| speculation     | MTP `--spec-type draft-mtp`, n=3 | MTP k=3, probabilistic drafts, 40k draft vocab                                                  |
| serving         | `--parallel 1`                   | `--max-num-seqs 8`                                                                              |

Both engines benched **standalone** — no proxy, no other clients. My first
llama.cpp reading went through my model proxy while an agent was chatting, and
the interleaved traffic silently halved one run (49 → 25 tok/s between two
identical requests). Bench engines directly or your numbers measure your
traffic, not your engine.

Harness for both: `vllm bench serve` with the syv-ai repo's
`prompts_real.jsonl` (8 realistic mixed prompts, 1,024-token answers). Yes, it
benches llama-server fine — it's just an OpenAI-compatible client; point
`--model` at any local directory with the right tokenizer.

## How the speed happens

None of this is stock `vllm serve`. The syv-ai repo's single-user numbers come
from a stack of deliberate moves (all in its `patches/` and `quant_*.py`; the
repo's README documents each with measurements):

- **Requantize what the public quant skipped.** Qwen3.8-27B has _untied_
  embeddings, so W4A16 quants ship two separate 2.5 GB bf16 matrices
  (embed_tokens and lm_head) that nobody touched. Requantizing both to int8
  frees ~2.6 GB of VRAM for KV — with a two-line vLLM patch to actually use
  the dequant-on-gather kernel that already existed.
- **fp16 recurrent state.** 48 of the 64 layers are Gated DeltaNet with a
  fixed per-request recurrent state that Qwen's config asks for in fp32
  (~150 MB per request). fp16 halves the footprint and the traffic;
  perplexity unchanged.
- **Make speculative drafts cheap enough that four pay off.** The shipped MTP
  draft module is 850 MB bf16 and each draft runs the full 248k-row lm_head.
  Their fix: int4-GPTQ the drafter _and_ the lm_head (calibrated on the
  model's own hidden states), and — the single biggest win — score drafts
  against a **40k-token draft vocabulary counted over the model's own
  outputs** (97.5% coverage of what it actually generates) instead of web
  text. A draft drops from ~3 ms to under 1 ms.
- **Unclog the verify step.** FlashAttention-2 only splits KV across SMs for
  single-query requests; the 5-query MTP verify step left most of the 3090
  idle. A small Triton split-KV kernel plus a sort-free top-k sampler patch
  buy another few percent.

What I verified is the _result_ of that stack, end to end: the numbers in the
table above are my card running their code, plus the two conditions they
didn't publish — 350 W, and a desktop sharing the GPU.

## The quality side

The W4A16 AutoRound quant measures ~lossless against BF16 upstream
([dbirks' evals](https://huggingface.co/dbirks/Qwen3.8-27B-W4A16-AutoRound), and
syv-ai's IFBench/GSM8K battery for the full serving stack: −1 point / 96.5%).
The UD-Q3_K_XL I run on llama.cpp is a visibly lossier quant. So the faster
engine also serves the better weights — there is no speed/quality trade here.

In my own (private) agent-eval suite, Qwen3.8-27B beat both KAT-Coder-V2.5-35B
and a cloud deepseek-v4-flash baseline across code, reasoning, long-context and
tool-selection cases. Different harness, same conclusion: this is the best
sub-30B local model I've had on this card.

## Vision survived the quant — but the launch script hides it

Qwen3.8-27B is a VL model. The syv-ai launch script passes
`--language-model-only`, which drops the vision tower to save VRAM — so image
requests get refused with `At most 0 image(s) may be provided`, and it _looks_
like the quant lost vision. It didn't: serving the same W4A16 checkpoint with
plain `vllm serve` (no `--language-model-only`, 64k context to make room), the
model read my test image exactly. One 27B covers code, agents and images in
24 GB.

## What I'd deploy

vLLM (`CTX=long`, vision on) as the resident model for everything interactive
and agentic — the concurrency alone retires a whole class of "agents fighting
over the GPU" incidents — with llama.cpp kept for what it is unbeatable at:
swapping arbitrary GGUFs the day they drop, and the occasional 196k-context
outlier job.
